Solar basics

How do solar panels work?

Solar panels turn daylight into electricity your home can use straight away. Here's the whole journey in plain English — from the roof to your sockets, your battery and the grid.

Updated July 2026·5 min read·Written by the Spark team

Key takeaways
  • ✓Solar panels convert daylight into direct current (DC) electricity — no direct sun required.
  • ✓An inverter turns that into the alternating current (AC) your home and appliances use.
  • ✓Your home uses solar first; a battery stores the surplus for later instead of exporting it cheaply.
  • ✓Panels still generate on cloudy days, just less than in bright sun.

The basics

A solar panel is made of many photovoltaic (PV) cells, usually silicon. When daylight hits a cell, it knocks electrons loose and creates a flow of electricity. It's the light that matters, not heat or direct sun — which is why panels work perfectly well in the UK climate.

Step by step

  1. Daylight hits the panels on your roof, generating direct current (DC) electricity.
  2. The inverter converts that DC into alternating current (AC) — the type your home uses.
  3. Your home uses it first, powering whatever is switched on at the time.
  4. Surplus is stored in a battery, if you have one, for use later.
  5. Any remaining surplus is exported to the grid, and you're paid for it.

What the inverter does

The inverter is the brain of the system. Its main job is converting DC to usable AC, but it also manages the flow — deciding when to power your home, charge your battery or export — and lets you monitor generation from an app.

Where a battery fits

Without a battery, you use solar only as it's generated and sell the rest back cheaply. A battery stores your cheap daytime energy so you can use it during the expensive evening peak — meaning you buy far less from the grid. Our battery guide covers sizing and payback.

Exporting to the grid

When you generate more than you can use or store, the surplus flows back to the grid. Under the Smart Export Guarantee, your supplier pays you for every unit you export.

Do they work on cloudy days?

Yes. Because panels run on daylight rather than direct sun, they keep generating when it's overcast — just at a lower rate than in bright conditions. Across a full UK year, that adds up to a substantial amount of free electricity.
